SMT (Satisfiability Modulo Theories) solvers are important tools that are used in many different fields like software engineering, verification, security, and artificial intelligence. People have noticed for a long time that there is no single solver or algorithm that is the best on all instances of a given SMT logic or a problem class. Because of this, modern SMT solvers like Z3 let users control strategies, so that users can customize a decision procedure for their own class of instances. A strategy is basically an algorithmic recipe that selects, sequences and parameterizes tactics. A tactic is a well-defined algorithmic proof rule or symbolic reasoning step that the solver provides. For example, propagate-values is a tactic in Z3 that propagates equalities, and sat and smt are tactics that wrap the main SAT and SMT solver in Z3. A strategy combines tactics to build a decision procedure, for example the strategy (if is-pb (then propagate-values sat) smt). What this strategy does is, given an input instance, it applies propagate-values and then sat if the instance is a pseudo-boolean problem (which is checked with is-pb), and otherwise it applies smt.

The default strategies of solvers are usually optimized for well-established benchmarks, for example the ones in the SMT-LIB library. But the scope of SMT applications is growing fast, and users often run into classes of instances that are specialized, evolving, and unprecedented. For these, the default strategies or the existing customized strategies might not work as well. So there is a need for new customized strategies that are designed specifically to handle the unique challenges of the users' specific problems efficiently. Usually this strategy customization has been done by human experts, who do a lot of experiments and benchmark analysis. But even for experts putting in lots of effort this is still hard, because tactics interact in intricate ways and the search space of possible strategies is very large.

There have been some early attempts to synthesize SMT strategies automatically. StratEVO uses evolutionary algorithms to search for an optimal strategy, and FastSMT uses imitation learning and decision tree learning to synthesize a tailored strategy. These methods are promising for automating strategy customization, but they have problems such as not being robust, limited interpretability, and long training times.

To deal with these problems, we introduce a new SMT strategy synthesis method that uses Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). MCTS is a heuristic search algorithm that is widely used in computer board game players as a lookahead planning algorithm. It became even more prominent after it was successfully integrated into the deep reinforcement learning systems AlphaGo and AlphaZero, which were groundbreaking, and where MCTS was used as a policy improvement operator. Recently MCTS has also been very successful as a standalone algorithm for solving complex symbolic or combinatorial search problems, for example in Khalil et al. and Sun et al.. Its main strengths are that it balances exploration and exploitation effectively and that it adapts to the nuances of different search problems, which makes it a great method for these kinds of hard tasks. Our work is the first one to apply MCTS to the SMT strategy synthesis problem.

## 1.1 The Strategy Synthesis Problem

The SMT strategy synthesis problem is to automatically find an optimal strategy that gives the best performance on a given benchmark set P. Performance is usually measured with metrics like the number of instances in P that are solved within a given wallclock timeout t. P is supposed to be a representative subset of a larger benchmark set Q that the user is interested in, and the expectation is that a strategy that does well on P will also generalize well to Q. Note that because the strategy search space is infinite and strategies are evaluated empirically, it is not practical to find a rigorously optimal solution. So our goal is to find a near-optimal solution in a reasonable search time, based on empirical measurements. In this research we focus on strategy synthesis for Z3, which is widely regarded as one of the most prevalent SMT solvers that are used today.

## 1.2 Our Contributions

1. Z3alpha, an MCTS-based strategy synthesizing solver: We present a new framework based on MCTS, which we call Z3alpha, for the SMT strategy synthesis problem. It automatically constructs solver strategies that are tailored to a given class of problem instances. As far as we know, Z3alpha is the first MCTS-based method for the SMT strategy synthesis problem.

2. Layered and staged MCTS: Strategy synthesis has some unique challenges that conventional MCTS alone cannot solve, so we develop two new heuristics on top of the MCTS framework, layered search and staged search. Layered search narrows the search space by treating some auxiliary tasks as independent search problems. Staged search splits the whole search problem into sequential sub-problems, so that results from the early stages can be used to speed up the search in the later stages. The two techniques work together to make the search for the optimal strategy more efficient and effective, which is important since evaluating strategies takes a lot of time.

3. Extensive experimental evaluation: We implemented Z3alpha on top of the leading SMT solver Z3. We did comprehensive experiments to evaluate its performance, comparing Z3alpha to the state-of-the-art (SOTA) synthesis tool FastSMT, and also to Z3's own handcrafted default strategy and to the CVC5 solver. The experiments covered instances from six different SMT logics (QF_{BV, LIA, LRA, NIA, NRA, S}), with a wide range of problem sizes and solver runtimes. In all of the experiments Z3alpha showed superior and robust performance. This shows the benefits of automatic strategy customization and promotes broader adoption of user-controllable strategies in the SMT community.

Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers [1] are key tools in many fields such as software engineering [2], verification [3], security [4], and artificial intelligence [5]. It has long been observed that no single solver or algorithm performs best across all instances of a given SMT logic or of a problem class. As a result, modern SMT solvers such as Z3 [6] offer user-controllable strategies [7], which let users customize a decision procedure for their class of instances. A strategy can be thought of as an algorithmic recipe for selecting, sequencing, and parameterizing tactics, where each tactic is a well-defined algorithmic proof rule or symbolic reasoning step provided by the solver. For example, propagate-values is a Z3 tactic that propagates equalities, while sat and smt are the tactic wrappers of the main SAT and SMT solver in Z3. A strategy builds a decision procedure by combining tactics, as in the example strategy (if is-pb (then propagate-values sat) smt). Given an input instance, this strategy applies propagate-values followed by sat if the instance is a pseudo-boolean problem (as checked using is-pb), and applies smt otherwise.

Default solver strategies are typically optimized for well-established benchmarks, such as those in the SMT-LIB library [8]. However, as the scope of SMT applications grows rapidly, users frequently encounter specialized, evolving, and unprecedented classes of instances, for which the default or existing customized strategies might not be as effective. There is therefore a need for new customized strategies designed specifically to address the unique challenges of users' specific problems efficiently. Traditionally, strategy customization has been done by human experts through extensive experimentation and benchmark analysis. Even with their expertise and effort, however, the task remains challenging because of the intricate interactions among tactics and the vast search space of potential strategies.

Early attempts have been made to synthesize SMT strategies automatically. For instance, StratEVO [9] searches for an optimal strategy using evolutionary algorithms, while FastSMT [10] synthesizes a tailored strategy using imitation learning and decision tree learning. Although these methods show promise in automating strategy customization, they suffer from a lack of robustness, limited interpretability, and extensive training times.

To address these issues, we introduce a new SMT strategy synthesis method based on Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). MCTS is a heuristic search algorithm widely applied in computer board game players as a lookahead planning algorithm [11]. Its prominence grew further after its successful integration into the groundbreaking deep reinforcement learning systems AlphaGo [12] and AlphaZero [13], where MCTS served as a policy improvement operator. Recently, MCTS has also shown remarkable success as a standalone algorithm for complex symbolic or combinatorial search problems, as in Khalil et al. [14] and Sun et al. [15]. Its key strengths, namely its ability to balance exploration and exploitation effectively and its adaptiveness to the nuances of varied search problems, make it an excellent fit for such challenging tasks. Our work is the first to apply MCTS to the SMT strategy synthesis problem.

## 1.1 The Strategy Synthesis Problem

The SMT strategy synthesis problem is defined as automatically identifying an optimal strategy that yields the best performance on a given benchmark set P. Satisfiability Modulo Theories (SMT) solvers [1] are key tools in diverse fields such as software engineering [2], verification [3], security [4], and artificial intelligence [5]. It has long been observed that no single solver or algorithm excels across all instances of a given SMT logic or of a problem class. As a result, modern SMT solvers, such as Z3 [6], offer user-controllable strategies [7], enabling users to customize a decision procedure for their class of instances. A strategy can be thought of as an algorithmic recipe for selecting, sequencing, and parameterizing tactics. Each tactic is a well-defined algorithmic proof rule or symbolic reasoning step, provided by the solver. For example, propagate-values is a Z3 tactic that propagates equalities, while sat and smt are the tactic wrappers of the main SAT and SMT solver in Z3. A strategy builds a decision procedure by combining tactics, as shown in an exemplar strategy (if is-pb (then propagate-values sat) smt). This strategy specifies a solving algorithm that, given an input instance, applies propagate-values followed by sat if the instance is a pseudo-boolean problem (as checked using is-pb), or applies smt otherwise.

Default solver strategies are typically optimized for well-established benchmarks, such as those in the SMT-LIB library [8]. However, as the scope of SMT applications continues to grow rapidly, users frequently encounter specialized, evolving, and unprecedented classes of instances. In these scenarios, the default or the existing customized strategies might not be as effective. Consequently, there arises a need for novel customized strategies, specifically designed to efficiently address the unique challenges posed by users’ specific problems. Traditionally, this task of strategy customization has been undertaken by human experts through extensive experimentation and benchmark analysis. However, even with their expertise and efforts, the task remains challenging due to the intricate interactions among tactics and the vast search space for potential strategies.

Early attempts have been made to synthesize SMT strategies automatically. For instance, StratEVO [9] searches for an optimal strategy using evolutionary algorithms, while FastSMT [10] synthesizes a tailored strategy using imitation learning and decision tree learning techniques. While these methods show promise in automating strategy customization, they suffer from issues such as a lack of robustness, limited interpretability, and extensive training times.

To address these issues, we introduce a novel SMT strategy synthesis method that employs Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS). MCTS is a heuristic search algorithm, widely applied in computer board game players as a lookahead planning algorithm [11]. Its prominence further escalated following its successful integration into the groundbreaking deep reinforcement learning systems AlphaGo [12] and AlphaZero [13], where MCTS was employed as a policy improvement operator. Recently, MCTS has shown remarkable success as a standalone algorithm in solving complex symbolic or combinatorial search problems, as evidenced in Khalil et al. [14] and Sun et al. [15]. Its key strengths, including its ability to effectively balance exploration and exploitation and its adaptiveness to the nuances of varied search problems, make it an excellent method for such challenging tasks. Our work is the first to apply MCTS to the SMT strategy synthesis problem.
