<details><summary><b>Step 3 — partial_draft</b> (214 words)</summary>

````text
Alonzo Church introduced the lambda calculus in the 1930s, as part of his investigation into the foundations of mathematics. In 1935 the original system was shown to be logically inconsistent, when Stephen Kleene and J. B. Rosser developed the Kleene–Rosser paradox.

After that, in 1936 Church isolated just the part that was relevant to computation and published it; this is what is now called the untyped lambda calculus. In 1940 he also introduced a system that was computationally weaker but logically consistent, the simply typed lambda calculus.

The lambda calculus was only a formalism until the 1960s, when its relation to programming languages was clarified. Because of Richard Montague and other linguists who applied it to the semantics of natural language, it has started to have a respectable place in both linguistics and computer science.

## Origin of the λ symbol
- uncertain why Church chose Greek letter lambda (\lambda) for function-abstraction; partly Church's own conflicting explanations

- Dana Scott, various public lectures: asked John W. Addison Jr., Church's former student and son-in-law
- Addison's postcard to Church [quote: iota/epsilon, why lambda?]
- Church's reply: returned postcard with "eeny, meeny, miny, moe"
````

<details><summary><b>Step 4 — rough_draft</b> (400 words)</summary>

````text
The lambda calculus was introduced by the mathematician Alonzo Church in the 1930s, as part of an investigation he was doing into the foundations of mathematics. The original system turned out to be logically inconsistent, which was shown in 1935 when Stephen Kleene and J. B. Rosser developed the Kleene–Rosser paradox.

After this, in 1936 Church isolated the portion that was relevant to computation and published just that, and this is what is now called the untyped lambda calculus. In 1940 he also introduced a system that was computationally weaker, but logically consistent, which is known as the simply typed lambda calculus.

Up until the 1960s, when its relation to programming languages was clarified, the lambda calculus was only a formalism. Richard Montague and other linguists applied it in the semantics of natural language, and thanks to this the lambda calculus has begun to enjoy a respectable place in both linguistics and computer science.

## Origin of the λ symbol

It is somewhat uncertain why Church used the Greek letter lambda (\lambda) as the notation for function-abstraction in the lambda calculus, and part of the reason may be that Church himself gave conflicting explanations. Cardone and Hindley (2006) write:

> By the way, why did Church choose the notation " \lambda "? In [an unpublished 1964 letter to Harald Dickson] he stated clearly that it came from the notation " {\hat {x}} " used for class-abstraction by Whitehead and Russell, by first modifying " {\hat {x}} " to " \land x " to distinguish function-abstraction from class-abstraction, and then changing " \land " to " \lambda " for ease of printing.

> This origin was also reported in [Rosser, 1984, p.338]. On the other hand, in his later years Church told two enquirers that the choice was more accidental: a symbol was needed and \lambda just happened to be chosen.

Dana Scott has also talked about this question in various public lectures. Scott tells the story that he once asked Church's former student and son-in-law John W. Addison Jr. a question about where the lambda symbol came from, and Addison then wrote his father-in-law a postcard:

> Dear Professor Church,

> Russell had the iota operator, Hilbert had the epsilon operator. Why did you choose lambda for your operator?

Scott says that Church's whole response was to return the postcard with the following annotation: " eeny, meeny, miny, moe ".
````

````text
The lambda calculus was introduced by mathematician Alonzo Church in the 1930s as part of an investigation into the foundations of mathematics. [1] The original system was shown to be logically inconsistent in 1935, when Stephen Kleene and J. B. Rosser developed the Kleene–Rosser paradox. [2,3]

## Origin of the λ symbol

There is some uncertainty over why Church used the Greek letter lambda (\lambda) as the notation for function-abstraction in the lambda calculus, perhaps partly because of conflicting explanations by Church himself. According to Cardone and Hindley (2006):

> By the way, why did Church choose the notation " \lambda "? > This origin was also reported in [Rosser, 1984, p.338]. On the other hand, in his later years Church told two enquirers that the choice was more accidental: a symbol was needed and \lambda just happened to be chosen.

Dana Scott has also addressed this question in various public lectures. [8] Scott recounts that he once asked Church's former student and son-in-law John W. Addison Jr. about the origin of the lambda symbol, and Addison then wrote his father-in-law a postcard:

According to Scott, Church's entire response was to return the postcard with the annotation: " eeny, meeny, miny, moe ".
````
