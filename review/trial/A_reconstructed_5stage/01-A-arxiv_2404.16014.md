<details><summary><b>Step 2 — rough_draft</b> (566 words)</summary>

````text
Mechanistic interpretability research tries to explain how neural networks produce their outputs, in terms of the learned algorithms that are executed during a forward pass. A lot of work uses the fact that many concept representations seem to be linear. However finding the set of all the interpretable directions is a highly non-trivial problem. The classic approaches like interpreting neurons (i.e. directions in the standard basis) are not enough, since many neurons are polysemantic and they tend to activate for a range of different concepts that seem unrelated.

The superposition hypothesis gives a mechanistic explanation for these observations. The idea is that in an intermediate representation of dimension n, a model will encode M\gg n concepts as linear directions. The set of concepts and their directions is fixed across all inputs, but on any given input only a sparse subset of the concepts are active, which means there is not much simultaneous interference between these concepts (which are non-orthogonal). Motivated by the superposition hypothesis, recent work used sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to find sparse decompositions of the model activations in terms of an overcomplete basis, or dictionary.

SAEs look promising for this, but the L1 penalty that the prevailing training method uses to encourage sparsity also introduces biases, and these harm the accuracy of SAE reconstructions, because the loss can go down by trading off some reconstruction accuracy for lower L1. We call the existing training methodology the baseline SAE, which is defined fully in Section 2.1 - 2.2 and which borrows heavily from earlier work. In this paper we introduce a modification to the baseline SAE architecture, which we call a Gated SAE, and also an accompanying loss function, and this partially overcomes these limitations. The key insight is that we use separate affine transformations for (a) determining which dictionary elements to use in a reconstruction and (b) estimating the coefficients of the active elements, and we only apply the sparsity penalty to the first one. To avoid significantly increasing the parameter count and the inference-time compute of a Gated SAE compared to a baseline SAE of equivalent width, we share a subset of weights between the two transformations.

We evaluate Gated SAEs on several models, a one layer GELU activation language models, Pythia-2.8B and Gemma-7B, and on several sites within the models, MLP layer outputs, attention layer outputs, and residual stream activations. Across all of these models and sites we find that Gated SAEs are a Pareto improvement over baseline SAEs when training compute is held fixed (Fig. 1), meaning they give sparser decompositions at any level of reconstruction fidelity that you want. We also do some follow up ablations and investigations on a subset of the models and sites, to understand better how Gated SAEs and baseline SAEs differ.

So the key contributions of this work are that we:

1. Introduce the Gated SAE, which is a modification to the standard SAE architecture that decouples detecting which features are present from estimating how big they are (Section 3.2);

2. Show that Gated SAEs Pareto improve the trade-off between sparsity and reconstruction fidelity compared to baseline SAEs (Section 4.1);

3. Confirm that Gated SAEs overcome the shrinkage problem (Section 4.2), and that they outperform other methods which also address this problem (Section 5.1);

4. Give evidence from a small double-blind study that Gated SAE features are about as interpretable as baseline SAE features (Section 4.3).
````

````text
Mechanistic interpretability research aims to explain how neural networks produce outputs in terms of the learned algorithms executed during a forward pass [1,2]. Much work makes use of the fact that many concept representations appear to be linear [3,4,2,5]. However, finding the set of all interpretable directions is a highly non-trivial problem. Classic approaches, like interpreting neurons (i.e. directions in the standard basis) are insufficient, as many are polysemantic and tend to activate for a range of different seemingly unrelated concepts [6,7,8].

The superposition hypothesis [8] posits a mechanistic explanation for these observations: in an intermediate representation of dimension n, a model will encode M\gg n concepts as linear directions, where the set of concepts and their directions is fixed across all inputs, but on a given input only a sparse subset of concepts are active, ensuring that there is not much simultaneous interference [4] between these (non-orthogonal) concepts. Motivated by the superposition hypothesis, [9] and [10] recently used sparse autoencoders (SAEs; [11]) to find sparse decompositions of model activations in terms of an overcomplete basis, or dictionary [12].

Although SAEs show promise in this regard, the L1 penalty used in the prevailing training method to encourage sparsity also introduces biases that harm the accuracy of SAE reconstructions, as the loss can be decreased by trading-off some reconstruction accuracy for lower L1. We refer to this existing training methodology as the baseline SAE, defined fully in Section 2.1 - 2.2 and which borrows heavily from [9]. In this paper, we introduce a modification to the baseline SAE architecture – a Gated SAE -- along with an accompanying loss function, which partially overcomes these limitations. Our key insight is to use separate affine transformations for (a) determining which dictionary elements to use in a reconstruction and (b) estimating the coefficients of active elements, and to apply the sparsity penalty only to the former task. We share a subset of weights between these transformations to avoid significantly increasing the parameter count and inference-time compute requirements of a Gated SAE compared to a baseline SAE of equivalent width.

We evaluate Gated SAEs on multiple models: a one layer GELU activation language models [13], Pythia-2.8B [14] and Gemma-7B [15], and on multiple sites within models: MLP layer outputs, attention layer outputs, and residual stream activations. Across these models and sites, we find Gated SAEs to be a Pareto improvement over baseline SAEs holding training compute fixed (Fig. 1): they yield sparser decompositions at any desired level of reconstruction fidelity. We also conduct further follow up ablations and investigations on a subset of these models and sites to better understand the differences between Gated SAEs and baseline SAEs.

Overall, the key contributions of this work are that we:

- 1. Introduce the Gated SAE, a modification to the standard SAE architecture that decouples detection of which features are present from estimating their magnitudes (Section 3.2);

- 2. Show that Gated SAEs Pareto improve the sparsity and reconstruction fidelity trade-off, compared to baseline SAEs (Section 4.1);

- 3. Confirm that Gated SAEs overcome the shrinkage problem (Section 4.2), while outperforming other methods that also address this problem (Section 5.1);

- 4. Provide evidence from a small double-blind study that Gated SAE features are comparably interpretable to baseline SAE features (Section 4.3).
````
