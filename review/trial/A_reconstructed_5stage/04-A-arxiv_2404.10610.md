<details><summary><b>Step 2 — rough_draft</b> (1152 words)</summary>

````text
Residential proxies (RESIPs) are web proxies that are located in residential networks or in cellular networks. Previous studies have shown that RESIPs, compared to traditional web proxies like VPNs and open web proxies, tend to have a much larger scale and also a wider distribution. Because the scale is so large, previous studies have looked into how RESIP services recruit RESIPs, and this led to discovering multiple recruitment channels that are controversial or even malicious, for example installing potentially unwanted programs (PUPs) on Windows devices, distributing mobile proxy apps that have ambiguous user consent prompts, etc.

There has also been research to understand the usage (abuse) of RESIPs. Mi, et al. used traffic logs of PUPs that were confirmed to serve as RESIPs and discovered malicious RESIP traffic of multiple categories, like advertisement frauds and blackhat search engine optimization (SEO). But it is not clear if the PUP traffic logs are only RESIP traffic, because these PUPs can have other malicious functionalities at the same time, and these could also contribute to the malicious traffic logs. Also, RESIP traffic relayed by mobile proxy apps had been captured and analyzed, and suspicious traffic of similar categories was observed in it. However the authors did not specify the scale of the RESIP traffic they captured, and they did not release the RESIP traffic to the public. Since RESIPs are abused in various malicious activities, there have been further efforts to detect RESIP traffic. Some studies targeted traffic flows that are relayed by RESIPs, which are called relayed flows, and others focused on the tunnel connections between a RESIP node and a RESIP gateway server, which are called tunnel flows. However none of these methodologies was evaluated on wild RESIP traffic.

This paper goes further than these previous studies and it makes contributions in several aspects. First, we collected the largest-ever dataset of RESIP traffic, which is over 3TB, and it has over 116 million traffic flows going to traffic destinations of 188K IP addresses and 116K fully qualified domain names. Also, this dataset of wild RESIP traffic gives us the opportunity to see if it is feasible to apply machine learning to detecting both relayed flows and tunnel flows. We built multiple machine learning classifiers and they got very good performance. Our relayed flow classifier got a recall of 88.80% and a precision of 96.20%, and our tunnel flow classifier got 91.39% recall and 95.71% precision. We also did an in-depth analysis of the traffic flows relayed by RESIPs, and the analysis gave multiple novel findings about how RESIPs are abused in malicious activities.

To do this study we had to design and implement a set of novel methodologies. The first challenge we had was how to collect RESIP traffic at scale, especially since RESIP services are known to keep their RESIPs as black boxes. We observe that some RESIP providers are also operating bandwidth sharing (BS) as a service at the same time. The representative BS providers we studied include PacketStream, IPRoyal, and Honeygain. A BS service is offered to monetize the idle bandwidth on a BS node (e.g., a laptop device), and the node operator gets paid in proportion to how much bandwidth the node contributed. We ran BS nodes in preliminary experiments and analyzed the traffic that came out, and we confirmed that the bandwidth provided by BS nodes is used only to relay RESIP traffic. So BS nodes are used exclusively as RESIP nodes. Therefore we collected RESIP traffic by deploying BS nodes at scale. While doing this we also had to address other challenges, like how to trigger effective BS (RESIP) traffic and how to deploy BS nodes and collect the traffic in a scalable way, and together these lead to a general RESIP traffic collector. Then we built a RESIP traffic analyzer that processes RESIP traffic efficiently and locates the inherent security risks effectively. Our in-depth analysis of RESIP traffic found a set of novel and concerning security findings, which we highlight below. Since the security risks of RESIP traffic are concerning, we also designed a set of robust features and built multiple machine learning classifiers, which enable early-time detection of both relayed flows and tunnel flows with high precision.

Below we summarize the key findings and contributions of this study.

First, we observe for the first time that RESIPs have been used extensively to masquerade attackers as local residents when they visit sensitive websites that are operated by government agencies, military agencies, and organizations in other public sectors. In total we observed suspicious traffic flows to 73 different security-sensitive websites. These websites belong to diverse categories, for example industrial control systems, airport control systems, watering control systems, remote desktops inside government agencies, and various military websites. Of these websites, 31% are operated by governments, 18% are for military agencies, and another 21% are for other organizations in the public sector, e.g., hospitals, education agencies, railway management companies, and research agencies. We also observe a strong correlation between the country of our RESIP nodes and the country where these sensitive websites are hosted.

Also for the first time, we confirm that RESIPs have been used to relay a large volume of email spamming activities and also suspicious email retrieving activities. The email spamming activities involve 465K sender email addresses, 2.29 million recipient email addresses, and 549K unique spam messages. We also observe that email spammers use various evasion techniques, e.g., text paraphrasing, complicated combinations of character encodings, email encodings, homoglyphs, etc.

Next, we built a set of machine learning classifiers to enable early-time detection of RESIP traffic, and they show good performance. Our transformer-based classifiers remove the burden of manual feature engineering, and they also get high detection performance while only taking in the first 5 packets of each traffic flow. For example, the transformer-based model for relayed traffic classification got a recall of 93.04% and a precision of 92.87%. Our feature-based models on the other hand have better explainability, and their performance is also decent. For example, when only the first 4 upstream packets are considered, our feature-based model for tunnel flow classification can get a recall of 91.94% and a precision of 94.87%. As far as we know, this is the first work that applies machine learning to detecting RESIP traffic.

Our contributions are summarized below.

\bullet We designed and implemented a novel methodology to collect RESIP traffic at a large scale and to analyze RESIP traffic for security risks.

\bullet Using the RESIP traffic collector, we collected the largest-ever dataset of wild RESIP traffic. This traffic dataset will be available upon request, so that it fosters future research but also avoids potential security or privacy risks.

\bullet Using the RESIP traffic analyzer, we identified multiple novel and concerning security findings about the malicious usage of RESIPs.

\bullet We demonstrated for the first time that it is feasible to apply machine learning to detecting RESIP traffic.
````
