Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs) have had a lot of applications throughout academia recently. Open-source software libraries like TensorFlow and Keras have been developed, and these make it possible to train and test ANNs quickly for many different purposes. In particular there have been a steady stream of applications to cryptanalysis questions in historical cryptology in the last 10 years, for example there are ANNs that have been created for cipher detection among sets of classical ciphers. In this paper we build an ANN for another important cryptanalysis question in historical cryptology, which is how we can accurately predict the key length of a Vigenère cipher.

The Vigenère cipher is one of the most well-studied historical ciphers. Its development can be traced back to the mid 1400s and several cryptologists were involved in it. A detailed description of the history of the Vigenère cipher can be found in Chapter 4 of another source. In this cipher a keyword of length k is used to give a sequence of k shifts, and these shifts are used repeatedly in order to encrypt. If a cryptanalyst knows that the key length is k, they can partition the ciphertext into k cosets, and each coset contains letters that were encrypted with the same shift (or the same alphabet if it is an arbitrary polyalphabetic substitution cipher). If these cosets are large enough, it is straightforward to finish breaking the cipher using frequency analysis, and there are examples that implement this technique. So it is important to have accurate and efficient algorithms for finding the key length first.

Several algorithms have been developed to predict the key length, and we review them in detail in Section 2. Historically the two most well known techniques are the Babbage–Kasiski test and the index of coincidence, and these have been applied for over a hundred years. More recently a series of papers introduced the twist-based algorithms, which are new approaches to predicting the key length. Barr–Simoson introduced the original twist algorithm. Park–Kim–Cho–Yum gave an improved version which is called the twist + algorithm. Millichap–Yau–Pate–Carns made further modifications to build the twist ++ algorithm. Each of these algorithms has its own strengths and weaknesses for a wide variety of key lengths and text lengths. So it is natural to build an ANN for key length prediction that uses these algorithms, in the hope of highlighting their individual strengths and mitigating their weaknesses.

In this paper we give an accurate model for predicting the key length of a Vigenère cipher for a large range of key lengths and text lengths. The ANN uses both classical tools (the index of coincidence in a variety of formats) and much more recent algorithms (the twist-based algorithms), and also a few other features. The exact features of our model are given in Section 3.3. We compare the accuracy with the index of coincidence and the twist-based algorithms in Table 2, and this clearly shows the superior accuracy of our ANN for a variety of text lengths.

The paper is organized like this. Section 2 reviews a variety of well-established methods from the literature for predicting the key length of a Vigenère cipher and analyzes their strengths and weaknesses. We tested all of these tools as potential features for our ANN. Section 3 discusses how we created, trained, and evaluated an ANN for finding the key length of the Vigenère cipher.
