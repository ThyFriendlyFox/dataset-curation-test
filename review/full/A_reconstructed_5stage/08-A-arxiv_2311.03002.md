<details><summary><b>Step 2 — rough_draft</b> (743 words)</summary>

````text
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are seen as the 'gold standard' for estimating treatment effects, in medical research and also in the social sciences. If they are done properly, RCTs give control over who gets the treatment, and so confounding factors are removed. But this advantage comes with several shortcomings. It is usually expensive to recruit enough patients and to collect their data, both in time and in money, so in general we are limited to smaller sample sizes. This constraint on study size can get even worse for rare diseases, and for serious diseases RCTs also have ethical challenges because no one can be left untreated. Single-arm trials are a way out of these problems, but they need external control data, which means they are not randomized anymore.

The control observations usually come from the control arms of historical RCTs, or from so-called real-world data (RWD), in the form of electronic health records (EHR), which are a promising source for them. In medicine, EHR means records that are collected during regular healthcare and hospital visits, and not as part of some specific RCT study. How far RWD and RCT can replace and complement each other is still an open research question, theoretically and also practically. There are large-scale projects going on right now that evaluate trial replicability with RWD data, to better understand the strengths and limitations of each.

In this work we look at augmenting single-arm trials with external controls. To estimate treatment effects we have to assume the two groups have some intrinsic similarity. But since they come from different sources, their covariate specifics will be different, for example in whether medical history records are available, how broad the lab measurements are, measurement precision, variance in demographics, and so on. We focus on three problems that come up in this setting. The first is that the covariate distributions of RCT data and RWD are different, so there is limited overlap between the two sets of observations, and this has to be overcome to get a reliable treatment effect estimate. The second is that most prior work in machine learning assumes access to treatment outcome information for both the single-arm trial patients and the external controls. We extend this by also considering the task of finding a suitable set of external controls pre-treatment (i.e. before the single-arm group gets the treatment). Here we have pre-treatment covariates for both groups and outcome values only for the external control group. This setup is very relevant because no information from the trial results leaks to the model while it is learning and matching the patients. Without new techniques like this, including the trial results when inferring the latent variable model could bias later statistical estimates that rely on the learned latent representation. The third problem is that real-world data will usually have missing measurements, and their non-random patterns have to be accounted for and modeled properly.

We introduce a latent variable model that infers group-specific and also shared identifiable latent representations, which are then used for treatment effect estimation. The group-specific ones let us explain away the specifics that are unique to the treatment subset and to the control subset. The shared representation gives a compressed latent space for treatment effect estimation and for patient matching, i.e., selecting the subset of control patients that are most similar to the treated group. This is different from the popular approach in the medical literature, which uses various kinds of propensity score estimators that do not infer such a space before estimating a matching score. Figure 1 shows the problem setting and our approach.

So in this work we consider estimating treatment effects from single-arm trials with external controls, and we also introduce an extra scenario where outcome information for the treatment group is not available during inference. We contribute

- (i) a principled way to handle these tasks using amortized latent-variable models with identifiability guarantees, which can infer a predictive latent space between two different covariate distributions for treatment effect prediction afterwards, and at the same time model structured missingness patterns;

- (ii) an extensive ablation study which shows that our method is competitive when outcome information is available for both treated and control groups, and also when it is available only for the control group. The method improves on prior work on several variations of a semi-synthetic benchmark and on a curated large-scale data set that combines data from a published RCT study with real-world EHRs.
````

````text
Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are the ‘gold standard’ in medical research and social sciences for the estimation of treatment effects. When conducted properly, RCTs provide control over the treatment assignment and therefore the removal of confounding factors. However, this advantage comes with the price of several shortcomings. Recruiting a sufficient number of patients and collecting their data is usually a costly endeavor both in terms of time as well as financial investments, limiting us to smaller sample sizes in general. The study size constraint may further be exacerbated in the case of rare diseases, and RCTs also have ethical challenges in case of serious diseases when no one can be left untreated. Single-arm trials offer a way out of these predicaments at the price of requiring external control data, thus no longer being randomized.

These control observations are usually taken from the control arms of historical RCTs, or from so-called real-world data (RWD) in the form of electronic health records (EHR) are a promising source for these control observations. In the medical domain, EHR refers to records collected during regular healthcare and hospital visits and not as part of a specific RCT study. The degree to which RWD and RCT can replace and complement each other is an open research question, both theoretical [1,2] as well as practical [3]. Large-scale projects evaluating trial replicability with RWD data are currently ongoing to better understand their respective strengths and limitations [4,5].

In this work, we consider the task of augmenting single-arm trials with external controls [6,7,8]. To estimate treatment effects we have to assume that these two groups have some intrinsic similarity. Yet, given their different sources, their covariate specifics will vary, e.g., concerning the availability of medical history records, breadth of lab measurements, measurement precision, variance in the demographics, etc. We focus specifically on three problems that arise in this setting. First, due to the differences in the covariate distributions between data collected in RCT and RWD, there is limited overlap between the two sets of observations which has to be overcome to provide a reliable treatment effect estimate [9]. Second, most prior work in the machine learning literature [10,11,12,13,14,15] has focused on the assumption of having access to treatment outcome information from both the single-arm trial patients as well as the external controls. We extend this assumption by considering the additional task of having to find a suitable set of external controls pre-treatment (i.e. before the treatment has been administered to the single-arm group). Here, we have access to pre-treatment covariates for both groups as well as the outcome values for the external control group. This setup is highly relevant as it avoids leaking any information from the trial results to the model during the model learning and matching of the patients. Without such new techniques, the inclusion of trial results while inferring the latent variable model could bias further statistical estimates that rely on the learned latent representation. Finally, we have to account for the fact that real-world data will usually include missing measurements whose non-random patterns have to be accounted for and modeled properly.

We introduce a latent variable model to infer group-specific as well as shared identifiable latent representations for subsequent treatment effect estimation. The former allows us to explain away specifics that are unique to the treatment and the control subsets, respectively, while a shared representation provides a compressed latent space for treatment effect estimation as well as patient matching, i.e., the selection of a subset of control patients that are most similar to the treated group. This differs from the popular approach in the medical literature that relies on various forms of propensity score estimators [16], which do not infer such a space prior to estimating a matching score. The problem setting and our approach are conceptualized in Figure 1.

In this work, we consider the task of estimating treatment effects from single-arm trials with external controls and also introduce an additional scenario where outcome information is not available for the treatment group during inference. We contribute

- (i) a principled way of handling these tasks via amortized latent-variable models with identifiability guarantees that can infer a predictive latent space between two different covariate distributions for subsequent treatment effect prediction, and simultaneously model structured missingness patterns;

- (ii) an extensive ablation study demonstrating that our method is competitive if outcome information is available for both treated and control groups, as well as if it is available only for the latter. The method improves upon prior work on several variations of a semi-synthetic benchmark as well as on a curated large-scale data set that combines data from a published RCT study [17] with real-world EHRs.
````
