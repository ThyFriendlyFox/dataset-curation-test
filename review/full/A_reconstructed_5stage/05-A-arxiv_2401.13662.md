<details><summary><b>Step 2 — rough_draft</b> (497 words)</summary>

````text
Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a set of methods where an agent learns how to act optimally in a given environment so that it maximizes some reward signal. Unlike other methods such as dynamic programming, RL learns the optimal policy, which is what dictates the optimal behavior, through trial and error by interacting with the environment. Most of the early successful applications of RL used value-based methods, and these estimate the expected future rewards to inform the decisions of the agent. But these methods only optimize the true objective of learning an optimal policy indirectly and they are not trivial to apply when the action space is continuous.

In this work we discuss policy gradient algorithms as an alternative approach, which tries to learn an optimal policy directly. Policy gradient algorithms are not new at all, but this subfield only got traction in recent years after deep RL emerged and various powerful algorithms were developed. Deep RL is a subfield of RL that uses neural networks and other deep learning methods. Interest in policy gradient algorithms has increased because this class of algorithms has several appealing properties. They can be used natively in continuous action spaces, and they can still be applied to discrete spaces too. Unlike value-based methods, policy gradient algorithms learn stochastic policies inherently. This gives smoother search spaces and partly remedies the exploration problem, which is the problem of having to acquire knowledge about the environment in order to optimize the policy. In some settings the optimal policy may also be stochastic itself. Lastly, policy gradient methods allow smoother changes in the policy during learning, and this may lead to better convergence properties.

Our goal is to give a holistic overview of policy gradient algorithms. We limit the scope to on-policy algorithms, which are defined in Section 2. So we exclude some popular algorithms, including DDPG, TD3 and SAC. Figure 1 gives an overview of RL and the subfields we cover. Our contributions are:

- We give a comprehensive introduction to the theoretical foundations of policy gradient algorithms, and this includes a detailed proof of the continuous version of the Policy Gradient Theorem.

- We derive the most prominent policy gradient algorithms and compare them, and we provide high quality pseudocode so that they are easier to understand.

- We release competitive implementations of these algorithms. This includes, as far as we know, the first publicly available V-MPO implementation that shows performance on par with the results in the original paper.

The rest of the paper is organized like this. Section 2 introduces fundamental definitions in RL and also gives an overview of deep learning. Section 3 derives the theoretical foundations of policy gradient algorithms, with a special focus on the proof of the Policy Gradient Theorem, and in Section 4 we use it to construct several existing practical algorithms. Section 5 discusses convergence results from the literature. Section 6 presents the results of our numerical experiments that compare the discussed algorithms. Section 7 concludes.
````

````text
Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a powerful set of methods for an agent to learn how to act optimally in a given environment to maximize some reward signal. In contrast to other methods such as dynamic programming, RL achieves this task of learning an optimal policy, which dictates the optimal behavior, via a trial-and-error process of interacting with the environment [1]. Most early successful applications of RL use value-based methods (e.g., [2,3,4]), which estimate the expected future rewards to inform the agent’s decisions. However, these methods only indirectly optimize the true objective of learning an optimal policy [5] and are non-trivial to apply in settings with continuous action spaces [1].

In this work, we discuss policy gradient algorithms [1] as an alternative approach, which aims to directly learn an optimal policy. Policy gradient algorithms are by no means new [6,7,8,9], but this subfield only gained traction in recent years following the emergence of deep RL [4] with the development of various powerful algorithms (e.g., [10,11,12]). Deep RL is a subfield of RL, which uses neural networks and other deep learning methods. The increased interest in policy gradient algorithms is due to several appealing properties of this class of algorithms. They can be used natively in continuous action spaces without compromising the applicability to discrete spaces [13]. In contrast to value-based methods, policy gradient algorithms inherently learn stochastic policies, which results in smoother search spaces and partly remedies the exploration problem of having to acquire knowledge about the environment in order to optimize the policy [1,13]. In some settings, the optimal policy may also be stochastic itself [1]. Lastly, policy gradient methods enable smoother changes in the policy during the learning process, which may result in better convergence properties [13].

Our goal is to present a holistic overview of policy gradient algorithms. In doing so, we limit the scope to on-policy algorithms, which we will define in Section 2. Thus, we exclude some popular algorithms including DDPG [14], TD3 [15] and SAC [16]. See Figure 1 for an overview of RL and the subfields we cover. Our contributions are as follows:

- • We give a comprehensive introduction to the theoretical foundations of policy gradient algorithms including a detailed proof of the continuous version of the Policy Gradient Theorem.

- • We derive and compare the most prominent policy gradient algorithms and provide high quality pseudocode to facilitate understanding.

- • We release competitive implementations of these algorithms, including the, to the best of our knowledge, first publicly available V-MPO implementation displaying performance on par with the results in the original paper.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 introduces fundamental definitions in RL as well as an overview of deep learning. Section 3 derives the theoretical foundations of policy gradient algorithms with a special focus on proving the Policy Gradient Theorem, based on which we will construct several existing practical algorithms in Section 4. In Section 5, we discuss convergence results from literature. Section 6, presents the results of our numerical experiments comparing the discussed algorithms. Section 7 concludes.
````
