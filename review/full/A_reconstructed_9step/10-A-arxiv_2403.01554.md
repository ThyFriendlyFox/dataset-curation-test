````text
We look at the problem of online supervised learning with modern deep neural networks, which is sometimes also called online continual learning. We have a sequence of observations x_{t} (e.g. images) and prediction targets y_{t} (e.g. labels), and we want a predictive model p(y_{t}|x_{t},\mathcal{D}_{<t}) that minimizes the cumulative log-loss \mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log p(y_{t}|x_{t},\mathcal{D}_{<t}). Here \mathcal{D}_{<t}=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots(x_{t-1},y_{t-1})\} is the sequence of observations and prediction targets up to (including) t{-}1. This formulation is very general and many different approaches can be used to solve it. It is also well motivated in theory, and it pushes us towards models with a lot of good properties like fast adaptation, sample-efficient learning, and maintaining plasticity. Also, minimizing the cumulative next-step log-loss is a form of the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle. Appendix A explains this connection in more detail and points out some of its properties and benefits.

In online learning, traditionally the conditioning on previous examples comes from the learned model parameters \theta, and otherwise the model is independent across examples. So the general objective becomes \mathcal{L}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}-\log p(y_{t}|x_{t},\theta_{t-1}), where the model parameters are a function of the data seen before, \theta_{t-1}=\hat{\theta}(\mathcal{D}_{<t}). Often there are constraints on how the parameter estimator can access the previous data. For example, in the streaming online-learning scenario only the most recent example is used to update the parameters: \theta_{t}=\text{update-step}(\theta_{t-1},x_{t},y_{t}). For online gradient descent this becomes \theta_{t}=\theta_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{t}|x_{t},\theta_{t-1}). In the deep-learning community it is common to use experience replay or core-sets, which do multiple gradient steps on buffered examples to update the parameters. Recent work argues that storing all the previous data \mathcal{D}_{<t} is often not a big technical challenge, and that instead of limiting the replay-buffer size we should think about limits on compute. Following this, other work proposes to replay the sequence in-order from long-term storage.

Another line of work looks at approaches inspired by memory or retrieval. These methods often use pre-trained feature extractors and similarity measures to retrieve useful information from examples seen before. For example, one of these works applies approximate online k-nearest neighbor (kNN) to CLOC, which is a large-scale and real-world online geo-localization sequence. For each new observation x_{t} they retrieve a similar example (x_{i},y_{i}) with i{<}t, and predict the label based on y_{i}.

- Our hybrid
  - transformer p(y_{t+1}|x_{t+1},\mathcal{D}_{(t-C)\cdots t},\theta), C most recent observations
  - online gradient descent on \{(x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots(x_{T},y_{T})\}
  - replay-streams: chronological replay, multi-epoch benefits, strict online next-step evaluation
- Motivation
  - gradient learning: stationary / slowly changing data, progress after millions of examples
  - transformers: short-term correlations in C most recent; in-context few-shot learning from meta-learning data
  - conjecture: in-context for sudden re-occurring changes; SGD for long sequences
- Contributions: empirical study of online transformers; pi transformer
- CLOC results: far exceed previous accuracies

Furthermore, minimizing the cumulative next-step log-loss is a form of the Minimum Description Length (MDL) principle; Appendix A explains this connection in more depth and highlights some of its properties and benefits.

Traditionally, in online learning, the conditioning on previous examples comes from the learned model parameters \theta, and the model is otherwise independent across examples. In the streaming online-learning scenario, for instance, only the most recent example is used to update the model parameters: \theta_{t}=\text{update-step}(\theta_{t-1},x_{t},y_{t}). For online gradient descent this simplifies to \theta_{t}=\theta_{t-1}+\alpha_{t}\,\nabla_{\theta}\log p(y_{t}|x_{t},\theta_{t-1}). In the deep-learning community, it is common to use experience replay or core-sets to perform multiple gradient steps on buffered examples when updating the parameters [1]. Recent work argues that storing all previous data \mathcal{D}_{<t} is often not a major technical challenge, and that limits on compute requirements should be considered instead of limits on the size of the replay-buffer [2]. To that end, [3] propose to replay the sequence in-order from long-term storage.

Another line of work considers memory- or retrieval-inspired approaches. These methods often rely on pre-trained feature extractors and similarity measures to retrieve relevant information from previously observed examples. [2], for example, apply approximate online k-nearest neighbor (kNN) to CLOC, a large-scale and real-world online geo-localization sequence. For each new observation x_{t} they retrieve a similar example (x_{i},y_{i}) with i{<}t, and make a label prediction based on y_{i}.

In this work we propose a hybrid approach: We use a transformer model p(y_{t+1}|x_{t+1},\mathcal{D}_{(t-C)\cdots t},\theta) that is explicitly conditioned on the C most recent observations while at the same time training the model with online gradient descent on the chronologically ordered sequence \{(x_{1},y_{1}),\cdots(x_{T},y_{T})\}. We use replay-streams [3] as a simple and effective way to implement chronological replay of previously observed examples – essentially re-introducing the benefits of multi-epoch training while strictly adhering to the online next-step evaluation protocol.

The motivation behind this approch is that gradient based learning has proven to be effective for stationary and slowly changing data. It promises steady learning progress even after seeing millions of examples. Explicitly conditioned transformer models on the other hand have demonstrated excellent performance when learning from short-term correlations within the window of the C most recent observations. Trained on data that exhibits meta-learning characteristics, transformers develop impressive in-context few-shot learning abilities. We conjecture that our approach can combine the complimentary strengths of these two approaches: In-context learning to enable rapid adaptation to re-occurring but potentially sudden changes in the data sequence; and parametric learning with SGD to enable sustained progress over long sequence lengths, far beyond the C most recent observations.

- • We empirically investigate the behaviour and properties of online trained transformer models when applied to non-stationary supervised image classification problems.

- • We propose the priviledged information (pi) transformer, a variant of the standard transformer architecture that is tailored to sequential supervised prediction problems. We show that this architecture often achieves faster, more stable, and better prediction performance than the standard architecture while at the same time being more compute efficient.

Our primary evaluation is on CLOC, a large scale and real-wold online continual learning benchmark. We show that both the standard transformer architecture, and especially the pi-tansformer, achieve excellent prediction performance and far exceed previously reported prediction accuracies.
