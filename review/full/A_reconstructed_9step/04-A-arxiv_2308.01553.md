<details><summary><b>Step 4 — rough_draft</b> (586 words)</summary>

````text
Datasets are very important in mobile robotics today, because the development of mapping and control algorithms depends heavily on them. To evaluate how well these algorithms perform, their poses are compared with a reference trajectory. In outdoor environments, RTS give the highest accuracy, since they measure reference trajectories with an uncertainty on the position in the range of millimeters. A total station comes from the field of surveying. It is an optic-based measurement instrument that can be aimed precisely at a given prismatic retro-reflector (we just call it a prism in the rest of this article). The total station is called robotic when it can track a prism automatically while the prism is moving. The RTS computes the position of the prism in its own local coordinate system, using the horizontal and vertical angles and the range between the RTS and the prism. If three prisms or more are attached to a robotic platform, the six- DOF pose of the platform can be computed, either with manual static measurement or with multiple RTS that continuously track three active prisms rigidly mounted on the same platform. Active prisms are available off the shelf only recently, and they give a unique light signature so that the RTS can identify the target automatically. Each prism is tracked by its own RTS, as shown in Figure 1. The distance between a RTS and its prism is found by EDM, and EDM is greatly impacted by weather conditions.

But to be useful for autonomous navigation research, evaluation protocols have to be used in many conditions and environments, for example in snowfalls, and these can increase the uncertainty of the ground truth trajectories. Besides the measurement noise of a single RTS, using multiple RTS means that time synchronization and extrinsic calibration are needed to fuse the data of all RTS in a common frame. Both the synchronization and the calibration have noises and uncertainties, and these need to be studied. Usually uncertainty analysis is not part of the evaluation pipelines of SLAM algorithms, because the most common metric to compare trajectories is the Euclidean distance. But current algorithms are developed to be accurate, so evaluating them without considering uncertainties will give biased results. Also, the state-of-the-art outdoor SLAM datasets do not have uncertainty estimations for their ground truth trajectories. Two factors could explain this: 1) the noises of ground truth trajectories are considered negligible, so the uncertainty is not computed, and 2) it can be complex to model the uncertainty of a reference system such as GNSS. Uncertainty models were developed for RTS, but they were never used to evaluate trajectories of mobile robots driven outdoors.

This work builds on our previous research where we developed an RTS setup for trajectory evaluations. In this paper we propose a method that models RTS uncertainties, with the objective of comparing six- DOF trajectories better. To do this, we carried out a MC method that includes five different sources of uncertainty related to multiple RTS measurements in outdoor environments. Then a GP interpolates these uncertainties over time, and they are propagated to the reference pose of a robot with another MC method. We present a detailed qualitative analysis of the different noise sources, and also of their impact on the final six- DOF trajectories. The experimental data used for the results was gathered during a whole year of deployments, with over 50\text{\,}\mathrm{km} of recorded trajectories in different weather conditions and environments. Our source code and our dataset are both freely available in our RTS_Project repository.
````

````text
In mobile robotics, the development of mapping and control algorithms currently relies heavily on datasets [1]. The performance of these algorithms is evaluated by comparing their poses with a reference trajectory. In outdoor environments, RTS provide the highest accuracy, measuring reference trajectories with a position uncertainty in the range of millimeters [2]. Coming from the field of surveying, a total station is an optic-based measurement instrument that can be precisely aimed at a given prismatic retro-reflector (simply called prism in the remainder of this article). A total station is robotic when it can automatically track a prism while this prism is in motion. The position of the prism is computed in the local coordinate system of the RTS from the horizontal and vertical angles, along with the range between the RTS and the prism. With three or more prisms attached to a robotic platform, its six- DOF pose can be computed through manual static measurement [3] or by using multiple RTS that continuously track three active prisms rigidly mounted on the same platform [4]. Active prisms have recently become available off the shelf and provide a unique light signature for automatic target identification by RTS. Each prism is tracked by its own assigned RTS, as shown in Figure 1. The distance between a RTS and its prism is determined by EDM, which is greatly impacted by weather conditions [5].

Yet, to be useful in autonomous navigation research, evaluation protocols need to be used in a variety of conditions and environments, such as snowfalls [6], which can increase the uncertainty of ground truth trajectories. In addition to the measurement noise of a single RTS, using multiple RTS requires time synchronization and extrinsic calibration to fuse the data of all RTS in a common frame [7]. Both the synchronization and the calibration carry noises and uncertainties that must be studied. Uncertainty analysis is usually not part of SLAM evaluation pipelines, as the most common metric for comparing trajectories is the Euclidean distance. However, since current algorithms are developed with the intention of being accurate, an evaluation that ignores uncertainties will lead to biased results. Moreover, state-of-the-art outdoor SLAM datasets lack uncertainty estimations for their ground truth trajectories. Two factors could explain this: 1) ground truth trajectory noises are considered negligible, so uncertainty is not computed; 2) modeling the uncertainty of a reference system such as GNSS can be complex. Uncertainty models were developed for RTS [8], but they have never been used for trajectory evaluations of mobile robots driven in outdoor environments.

This work builds on our previous research on an RTS setup for trajectory evaluations [4,7]. In this paper, we propose a method to model RTS uncertainties in order to better compare six- DOF trajectories. For that purpose, we carried out a MC method that includes five different sources of uncertainty related to multiple RTS measurements in outdoor environments. These uncertainties are then interpolated over time by a GP and propagated to the reference pose of a robot using another MC method. We present a detailed qualitative analysis of the different noise sources, as well as their impact on the resulting six- DOF trajectories. The experimental data used to compute the results was gathered during a whole year of deployments, with over 50\text{\,}\mathrm{km} of recorded trajectories in different weather conditions and environments. Both our source code and our dataset are freely available in our RTS_Project repository.
````
