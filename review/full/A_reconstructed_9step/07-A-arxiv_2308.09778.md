Visual reasoning is one of the general goals of vision-and-language models (VLMs). The recent advances in multi-modal vision-language models came from self-supervised pre-training objectives on web-scale datasets of image-text pairs. To evaluate them, people usually measure performance on downstream tasks, which include visual question answering (VQA), referring expression comprehension, image-to-text/text-to-image retrieval, image-text matching, or generative/auto-regressive tasks like image captioning. Several recent studies have shown that vision-language models lack fine-grain understanding of verbs, spatial relationship, and word order, and they lack general visio-linguistic compositionality, which is critical for compositional reasoning and generalization.

In this work we focus on the visual spatial reasoning task from the Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR) benchmark. We show qualitatively, with explainability tools, and quantitatively, with the performance of object detectors, that a significant weakness of multi-modal models is grounding the subjects and objects of the spatial relationships. Then we propose an alternative compositional ranking-based approach for understanding spatial clauses, which is built on top of the General Purpose Vision (GPV) multi-modal model that is pre-trained with object-level bounding box supervision and classification, visual question answering, and captioning. Our contributions are the following: i) We analyze the top-performing LXMERT model on the VSR benchmark and find problems with noun grounding and with the image-text matching (ITM) evaluation methodology. ii) We decompose spatial clauses into simpler primitives, grounding and localizing the subject and object with an encoder-decoder GPV model, and we train a separate, lightweight model that predicts their spatial relation. This gives a more traditional structured approach for combining the evidence about objects and spatial relations and ranking the spatial clauses. iii) We evaluate the approach on a subset of the VSR benchmark, and it shows that our strategy for spatial clause understanding is effective and that our ranking-based approach is suitable, since it avoids the biases of the VQA/GQA setting and gets better relative performance compared to random chance.

Visual reasoning is among the general goals of vision-and-language models (VLMs). Recent advances in multi-modal vision-language models were enabled by self-supervised pre-training objectives on web-scale datasets of image-text pairs. Their evaluation usually resorts to measuring performance on downstream tasks that include visual question answering (VQA), referring expression comprehension, image-to-text/text-to-image retrieval, image-text matching, or generative/auto-regressive tasks, like image captioning. Multiple recent studies have shown that vision-language models lack fine-grain understanding of verbs [1], spatial relationship [2,3], and word order [4], and lack general visio-linguistic compositionality [5], which is critical for compositional reasoning and generalization.

In this work, we focus on the visual spatial reasoning task considered in the Visual Spatial Reasoning (VSR) benchmark [2] and show qualitatively, using explainability tools [6], and quantitatively, using the performance of object detectors, that a significant weakness of multi-modal models is the ability to ground subjects and objects of the spatial relationships. We then propose an alternative compositional ranking-based approach for spatial clause understanding, built on top of the General Purpose Vision (GPV) multi-modal model [7] pre-trained with object-level bounding box supervision and classification, visual question answering, and captioning. Our contributions can be summarized as follows: i) We analyze the top-performing LXMERT model [8] on the VSR benchmark [2] and identify problems with noun grounding and the image-text matching (ITM) evaluation methodology. ii) We decompose spatial clauses into simpler primitives by grounding and localizing subject and object using an encoder-decoder GPV model [7], and train a separate, lightweight model for predicting their spatial relation. This yields a more traditional structured approach for combining the evidence about objects and spatial relations and ranking the spatial clauses. iii) The approach is evaluated on a subset of the VSR benchmark, demonstrating the effectiveness of our strategy for spatial clause understanding and the suitability of our ranking-based approach, which avoids the biases of the VQA/GQA setting and achieves better relative performance compared to random chance.
