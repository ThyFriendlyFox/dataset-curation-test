Being able to track the three-dimensional position and orientation of objects that are hidden from view is important for a lot of applications. In Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), for example, the user's own body can occlude their hands, and tracking is needed to decode gesture input and to give force feedback. In healthcare, tracking is used to guide medical instruments like capsules and catheters. In robotics it is used for things like magnetomicrometry and for tracking multi-arm robots. Magnetic tracking makes it possible to localize hidden objects precisely by using magnetic markers. The magnetic fields around the markers are measured with Hall sensors. There is a lot of interest in this technology because the field goes through many materials, including human tissue, without interference.

But tracking magnetic markers is not straightforward. Existing work distinguishes between active and passive magnetic markers. Active markers emit a magnetic signal using an integrated power source such as a battery, which gives a higher signal-to-noise ratio and also allows frequency filtering. The problem is that tracking active emitters needs either a wire to the tracked object or circuitry powered by a battery. Hashi et al. proposed semi-passive markers to get around this limitation. Here the tracked elements are passive, but they respond with a resonant signal when a nearby source excites them. Passive magnetic localization on the other hand is simpler, because the target only has to carry a permanent magnet, so no tethered connections or active electronics are needed on the moving parts. Our work mainly focuses on passive magnetic localization because it is simple and robust, but future work could maybe extend the ideas here to other kinds of magnetic markers.

To track a magnetic marker, we have to map the magnetic signals measured by one or more Hall sensors to the marker's 5-degree-of-freedom (5DoF) position and orientation (rotation). The simplest approach is to interpolate the reading intensity directly within the sensor array. In some earlier systems, this approach is used to track a stylus and a gaming object in a two-dimensional plane. In more complex setups, several sensors with a known spatial arrangement give an over-constrained system of equations, so the magnet's state has a unique solution. These triangulation methods are fast, but they are not robust to sensor noise and they are limited to the positional degrees of freedom.

A more robust approach, which also gives the rotation, is to minimize the difference between the sensor readings and a theoretical magnetic field based on a magnetic model. Several non-linear optimization algorithms have been proposed to do this. In one work, the authors computed the gradients analytically to make the optimization faster. In another work, the algorithm decouples the orientation of the marker from its position, which makes the calculation faster and gives guarantees about global minima. Still, these iterative approaches have big problems. First, gradient-descent algorithms need a lot of computation, so there is a trade-off between how precise the tracking is and its frequency. Second, iterative non-convex optimization can suffer from the solution not being unique, and it may converge to the wrong local minimum. Because of this, these methods depend a lot on their initialization.

These methods also assume that the magnetic model that predicts the theoretical field is valid. All of the methods above use the first term of the multi-pole series expansion that comes from Maxwell's equations. The magnetic dipole approximation assumes that all magnets are spherical, and it gives the simplest explicit expression of the magnetic field in terms of distance to the source. But this has a significant limitation. For magnets that are not spherical, the results are only reliable when the magnets are far from the sensor. And in the gradient descent optimization, a small error in approximating the magnetic field can lead to a large error in position.

- ML approaches
  - NN approximates non-linearities via multiply-add; no initial estimate; single inference; but building a large representative dataset is hard and slow
  - prior ML: in-vivo data collection at known locations, slow, not scalable; NN on synthetic dipole data keeps model inaccuracies; NNs on synthetic data for active markers only, not consistently better than iterative methods
- Our approach
  - FEM simulating complete Maxwell's equations; too expensive for real time but gives noise-free synthetic training data
  - FEM data used in other fields (mechanical deformations, elastoplasticity, material inspection, nano-structures), not magnetic tracking
  - axis-symmetric markers; coordinate transformation 2D -> 3D
- Results and contributions
  - simulation: beats iterative methods and dipole-data baseline
  - real world: cylindrical magnets, 5 DoF, Nvidia Jetson Nano; 4 mm, < 8 degrees, 75 Hz, 8 sensors
  - five contributions i)-v)

Recently, machine learning (ML) has become a useful way to avoid the computational cost of iterative methods. Machine learning, and especially Neural Networks (NN), is good at approximating non-linearities through sequential multiply-addition operations. It also doesn't need initial estimates, and it often gets reliable results with a single inference. The inference is fast, but making a large and representative training dataset so the model generalizes to unseen samples can be hard and take a long time.

In some works, ML is used to predict the locations of tracked magnets from the input of magnetic sensors. The training data is collected in-vivo, by putting magnets at different known locations and recording the sensor readings. This data collection takes a long time and does not scale to new scenarios and markers. Another work uses a neural network to track a magnetic marker, but trains it on synthetic data generated with the dipole model. This removes the need for iterative gradient-based solutions, but it does not fix the limitations of the underlying model, so the inaccuracies for non-spherical or closely located magnets are still there. Recently, the authors of one study and a second study showed neural networks trained on synthetic data for tracking active markers. We share conceptual goals with these works, but their results only apply to active emitters and they do not always outperform methods based on iterative optimization.

To deal with the data limitation problem, we use Finite Element Methods (FEM) to simulate the complete set of Maxwell's equations. FEM takes a lot of computation, especially with a fine mesh for accurate results, so it is not suitable for real-time modeling. But FEM can be used to generate noise-free synthetic datasets for training machine learning models. In other fields, synthetic datasets generated with FEM have been used successfully to train neural networks, though not for magnetic tracking. Examples are mechanical deformations, elastoplasticity, material inspection, and nano-structures. To reduce the computation time further while making the dataset bigger, we focus on axis-symmetric markers. We make this possible by introducing a coordinate transformation algorithm that uses the symmetric properties of our markers to convert FEM simulation results from 2D to 3D.

We evaluate our method both in simulation and in experiments. In simulation, our method outperforms iterative methods. We also compare our FEM-based approach to a method trained on data generated with the magnetic dipole model, and our method is significantly better than this baseline. In real-world experiments we test different cylindrical magnets with 5 degrees of freedom on a low-power portable Nvidia Jetson Nano, where sensing and tracking are done in parallel. We get an average error of 4 mm and an orientation error of less than 8 degrees, at an interactive rate of 75 Hz when using 8 sensors.

We show this by using a Nvidia Jetson Nano. v) We show that our approach is valid with in-silico and real-world evaluations. Together these contributions make magnetic tracking more accurate and efficient, and could lead to truly portable tracking devices.

Tracking the three-dimensional position and orientation of objects hidden from view is important for numerous applications. For instance, in Human-Computer Interaction (HCI), where the users' own body might occlude their hands, it involves decoding gesture input and delivering force feedback [1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8]. In healthcare, tracking plays a key role in guiding medical instruments such as capsules and catheters [9,10,11,12,13]. Similarly, in robotics, tracking is used for tasks such as magnetomicrometry [14] and tracking multi-arm robots [15]. Magnetic tracking enables precise localization of objects hidden from view by using magnetic markers, whose surrounding magnetic fields are measured with Hall sensors. The widespread interest in this technology comes from its ability to permeate through various materials, including human tissues, without interference.

However, tracking magnetic markers is not a straightforward task. Existing research distinguishes between active and passive magnetic markers. Active markers emit a magnetic signal through an integrated power source like a battery, which offers a higher signal-to-noise ratio and the advantage of frequency filtering. However, tracking active emitters requires either a wired connection to the tracked object or battery-powered circuitry [3,16]. Hashi et al. have proposed semi-passive markers to overcome this limitation [17]: while the tracked elements are passive, they respond with a resonant signal when excited by a nearby source. In contrast, passive magnetic localization is simpler, requiring only that the target be equipped with a permanent magnet and eliminating the need for tethered connections or active electronics on the moving elements [5,18,19]. Our work focuses on passive magnetic localization due to its simplicity and robustness; however, future work could extend the ideas presented here to other types of magnetic markers.

To track a magnetic marker, we need to map magnetic signals, measured by one or more Hall sensors, to the marker's 5-degree-of-freedom (5DoF) position and orientation (rotation). The most straightforward approach directly interpolates the reading intensity within the sensor array. In [20,21], this approach is used to track a stylus and a gaming object in a two-dimensional plane. In more complex configurations (e.g., [22,3,4]), multiple sensors with known spatial arrangements create an over-constrained system of equations, which ensures a unique solution for the magnet's state. These triangulation methods are fast, but they are not robust to sensor noise and are limited to positional degrees of freedom.

A more robust approach, which also infers rotation, minimizes the difference between the sensor readings and a theoretical magnetic field based on a magnetic model. Several non-linear optimization algorithms have been proposed for this [23,24]. In [19], the authors implemented an analytical computation of gradients to speed up the optimization. In [25], the algorithm decouples the marker's orientation from its position, which improves calculation speed and provides guarantees regarding global minima. Nonetheless, these iterative approaches come with significant challenges. First, gradient-descent algorithms are computationally demanding, which leads to a trade-off between tracking precision and frequency. Second, iterative non-convex optimization is susceptible to the non-uniqueness of the solution and can converge to wrong local minima. As a result, these methods are highly dependent on their initialization.

Furthermore, these methods assume that the magnetic model predicting the theoretical magnetic field is valid. All the methods mentioned above use the first term of the multi-pole series expansion derived from Maxwell's equations. The magnetic dipole approximation assumes all magnets to be spherical and provides the simplest explicit expression for the magnetic field with respect to distance to the source. However, this has a significant limitation: for non-spherical magnets, reliable results are only obtained when the magnets are far from the sensor [26]. Crucially, in a gradient descent optimization, a small error in approximating the magnetic field can lead to large positional discrepancies.

Recently, machine learning (ML) has emerged as a valuable way to avoid the computational burden of iterative methods. Machine learning, especially Neural Networks (NN), excels at approximating non-linearities through sequential multiply-addition operations. Additionally, it does not require initial estimates and often achieves reliable results with a single inference. While inference is fast, creating a large and representative training dataset that generalizes well to unseen samples can be challenging and time-consuming.

In [27,11], ML is used to predict the locations of tracked magnets from the input of magnetic sensors. The training data is collected in-vivo by placing magnets at various known locations and gathering the sensor readings, a lengthy process that does not scale to new scenarios and markers. [28] employ a neural network to track a magnetic marker, but train it on synthetic data generated by the dipole model. While this eliminates the need for iterative gradient-based solutions, it does not address the limitations of the underlying model, so inaccuracies for non-spherical or closely located magnets remain. Recently, the authors of [16] and [29] demonstrated neural networks trained on synthetic data for tracking active markers. Although we share conceptual goals with these works, their results apply only to active emitters and do not consistently outperform methods based on iterative optimization.

To address the data limitation, we use Finite Element Methods (FEM) to simulate the complete set of Maxwell's equations. FEM is computationally intensive, particularly with a fine mesh for accurate results, and is therefore unsuitable for real-time modeling. However, FEM can be used to generate noise-free synthetic datasets for training machine learning models. In various other fields, FEM-generated synthetic datasets have successfully been used to train neural networks, although not for magnetic tracking; examples include mechanical deformations [30], elastoplasticity [31], material inspection [32], and nano-structures [33]. To further reduce computation time while increasing dataset size, we focus on axis-symmetric markers. We enable this by introducing a coordinate transformation algorithm that exploits our markers' symmetric properties to convert FEM-simulation results from 2D to 3D.

We evaluate our method both in simulation and in experiments. In simulation, our method outperforms iterative methods. We also compare our FEM-based approach to a method trained on magnetic-dipole generated data, and our method significantly outperforms this baseline. In real-world experiments, we assess various cylindrical magnets with 5 degrees of freedom on a low-power portable Nvidia Jetson Nano, with sensing and tracking performed in parallel. We achieve an average error of 4 mm and an orientation error of less than 8 degrees, at an interactive rate of 75 Hz when using 8 sensors.

In summary, our work contributes to the magnetic tracking literature in five key ways: i) We propose a supervised learning approach that enables real-time tracking of arbitrary axis-symmetric magnets using Neural Networks, overcoming the computational limitations of previous iterative solutions by approximating the inverse function of the magnetic field. ii) To address data limitation, we use Finite Element Methods (FEM) to simulate Maxwell's equations, generating noise-free synthetic datasets for training machine learning models. iii) We introduce a coordinate transformation algorithm that exploits our markers' symmetric properties to convert FEM-simulation results from 2D to 3D, providing accurate high-resolution magnetic field data for training neural networks. iv) Thanks to the computational efficiency of our approach, we enable low-power and portable applications, which we demonstrate using a Nvidia Jetson Nano. v) We demonstrate the validity of our approach with in-silico and real-world evaluations. Together, these contributions enable more accurate and efficient magnetic tracking, with the potential for truly portable tracking devices.
