<details><summary><b>Step 2 — rough_draft</b> (615 words)</summary>

````text
Physics-informed machine learning is a versatile framework for emulating and inverting partial differential equations (PDEs) in physics, and it can be extended to other kinds of differential equations like stochastic differential equations, integro-differential equations, and fractional differential equations. This deep learning-based paradigm came from the physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which are targeted at learning a map x\mapsto u, where x is the coordinates of the collocation points sampled from the domain and u is the solution field. A built-in limitation of PINNs is that they do not generalise over variation of physical parameters, such as material properties, external sources, and initial and boundary conditions. This limitation motivated the physics-informed neural operators (PINOs), for example the deep operator networks (DeepONets) and the physics-informed Fourier neural operators (FNOs), which learn a map p(x)\mapsto u(x), where p(x) is any physical parameter of interest. So basically PINNs learn a map between two vector spaces and PINOs learn a map between two function spaces, and in many contexts a PINO becomes a PINN if p(x) is invariant.

The term "physics-informed" in PINNs and PINOs has a specific meaning, which is embedding the target PDE and its initial and boundary conditions into the loss function. This embedded information is supposed to help a neural network understand the underlying physics (and not just fit data) so it can get a lower generalisation error with less training data. To compute the loss functions for a PDE and for any of its non-Dirichlet boundary conditions, you need the high-order derivatives of the network output w.r.t. the coordinates. For example, bending of an elastic plate and the stream function for incompressible 2-D fluids both involve a fourth-order PDE. Also, the gradient of the PDE itself can be used as an even stronger regularisation, which makes the required orders of derivatives even higher. Automatic differentiation (AD) is a powerful tool for computing derivatives, and it is a pivotal technique behind deep learning today and one of the major things that made physics-informed machine learning possible.

There have been many technical advancements since modern PINNs were established, for example multi-scale and multi-frequency models, domain decomposition techniques, adaptive activation functions and data sampling, and embedding physics as hard constraints. Even with all these efforts, we think less attention has been paid to AD, especially since the coordinate derivatives in physics-informed machine learning are different in many ways from the well-known backpropagation (computing the gradient of the loss function w.r.t. network weights). We will give a comprehensive survey of related work in the next section.

In this paper we present a new algorithm that reshapes AD for operator learning, and it can reduce both GPU memory consumption and wall time for training by an order of magnitude. We call our algorithm Zero Coordinate Shift (ZCS). It introduces one scalar-valued leaf variable for each dimension, which simplifies the coordinate derivatives we want from "many-roots-many-leaves" to "one-root-many-leaves", so that reverse-mode AD, the most powerful mode, can be exploited as much as possible. The algorithm is at a lower level than the neural network (it is agnostic to the forward pass), so it puts no restrictions on data, point sampling or network architecture. Also, ZCS is straightforward to implement; we implemented it by extending the DeepXDE package with only a few overridden methods.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 gives a more detailed review of related work to further justify our motivation. Section 3 describes the theory and implementation of ZCS. Section 4 reports our experiments, including a benchmark analysis showing the scaling behaviours of ZCS and a few PDE operators learned without data. Section 5 describes the limitations we identified, and Section 6 concludes the paper.
````

````text
Physics-informed machine learning offers a versatile framework for the emulation and inversion of partial differential equations (PDEs) in physics [1,2], extensible to other types of differential equations such as stochastic differential equations [3], integro-differential equations [4], and fractional differential equations [5]. This deep learning-based paradigm has originated from the physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) [6,7,8,9], targeted at learning a map x\mapsto u, with x being the coordinates of the collocation points sampled from the domain and u the solution field. An innate limitation of PINNs is the absence of generalisability over the variation of physical parameters, such as material properties, external sources, and initial and boundary conditions. Such a limitation has motivated the physics-informed neural operators (PINOs), such as the deep operator networks (DeepONets) [10] and the physics-informed Fourier neural operators (FNOs) [11], which learn a map p(x)\mapsto u(x), with p(x) being any physical parameter of interest. In brief, PINNs learn a map between two vector spaces and PINOs a map between two function spaces; in many contexts, a PINO degenerates to a PINN given an invariant p(x).

The term “physics-informed” in PINNs and PINOs has a specific meaning: embedding the target PDE and its initial and boundary conditions into the loss function. Such embedded information is supposed to aid a neural network in understanding the underlying physics (rather than just fitting data) whereby to achieve a lower generalisation error with less training data. To compute the loss functions associated with a PDE and any of its non-Dirichlet boundary conditions, one needs the high-order derivatives of the network output w.r.t. the coordinates. For example, bending of an elastic plate and the stream function for incompressible 2-D fluids both involve a fourth-order PDE. Besides, the gradient of the PDE itself may serve as an even stronger regularisation [12], which further increments the required orders of derivatives. A powerful tool for derivative computation is automatic differentiation (AD), a pivotal technique behind today’s deep learning [13,14,15,16] and one of the major facilitators of physics-informed machine learning [1,2,9].

Technical advancements have been thriving since the establishment of mordern PINNs [8,9], e.g., multi-scale and multi-frequency models [17,18], domain decomposition techniques [19,20], adaptive activation functions [21] and data sampling [22,23], and embedding physics as hard constraints [24,25,26]. Despite such continuing efforts, we feel that less attention has been paid to AD, especially in view of that the coordinate derivatives in physics-informed machine learning differ from the well-known backpropagation (i.e., calculating the gradient of the loss function w.r.t. network weights) in many ways. A comprehensive survey of related work will be provided in the next section.

In this paper, we present a novel algorithm to reshape AD for operator learning, which can reduce both GPU memory consumption and wall time for training by an order of magnitude. Our algorithm, which we refer to as Zero Coordinate Shift (ZCS), inventively introduces one scalar-valued leaf variable for each dimension, simplifying the wanted coordinate derivatives from “many-roots-many-leaves” to “one-root-many-leaves” whereby the most powerful reverse-mode AD can be exploited to the maximum. Our algorithm lies at a lower level than the neural network (i.e., agnostic to the forward pass), imposing no restrictions on data, point sampling or network architecture. Furthermore, the implementation of ZCS is straightforward; our own implementation is by extending the DeepXDE package [27] with a very few overridden methods.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we provide a more insightful review of the related work for further justification of our motivation. In Section 3, we will describe the theory and implementation of ZCS. We report our experiments in Section 4, including a benchmark analysis exhibiting the scaling behaviours of ZCS and a few PDE operators learned without data. Identified limitations are then described in Section 5 before the paper being concluded in Section 6.
````
