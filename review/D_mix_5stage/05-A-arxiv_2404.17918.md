````text
Machine Translation (MT) has historically been under two influences that seem at first glance (prima facie) to contradict each other. One of the goals of MT research is to give means to convert sentences from any language into any other language. On the one hand, the generalization capabilities depend on our systems producing representations that are language agnostic. On the other hand, MT models should be good at encoding the specifics of the source languages. The first of these trends has deeply marked the field, and the concept of an 'interlingua' runs through most of the history of MT research, from early work to more recent work. The second trend has recently motivated the development of modular approaches, where the network parameters are tied specifically to a specific language.

So how can these two trends, which seem paradoxical, be reconciled? One approach that looks promising is to include fully-shared subnetworks in modular architectures, and especially bridge components. It has been argued that they foster language-independent representations and also zero-shot generalization capabilities. Our aim is to carefully assess whether modular architectures in general, and bridges, really do foster greater generalization capabilities.

To do this we study six architectures, and five of them are modular. We focus in particular on how they generalize, both to translation directions that were not seen and to novel domains. We find that modular systems still struggle to stay competitive with fully-shared MT systems in scenarios where not all translation directions are available, and this conclusion affects systems with and without fixed-size bridges equally. Encoder-sharing modular designs can rival or outperform non-modular settings in a wide range of scenarios, but all the other systems we study struggle in zero-shot and out-of-distribution conditions. This strongly questions the idea that fully-shared sub-networks in modular MT systems can improve their generalization capabilities.
